Overview

A stylized stealth adventure built in Unity

Mortimer: First Launch is a 3D adventure puzzle and stealth game in which players guide a raccoon named Mortimer on his attempt to build a rocket and reach the moon.

The game was developed by a team of five student developers over three months as part of JAMK University of Applied Sciences' Ticorporate Demo Lab.

I served as Product Owner while also leading much of the project's technical-art and VFX work.

Shaders & Materials

Building the game's stylized visual language

I developed four custom Shader Graph solutions: an outline shader, fresnel glow, human-character flipbook shader and animated object-shine shader.

I also created and maintained 112 materials, including 89 vegetation materials and 23 additional environment materials.

The project's water used a customized Stylized Water 2 setup with swamp-like rendering, displacement, ripples and splash interaction.

VFX & Presentation

Effects supporting the game's whimsical tone

I created ten distinct effects using Unity's VFX Graph and Particle System.

Four higher-impact effects used VFX Graph, while six simpler effects used the Particle System where the extra complexity was unnecessary.

I also developed the project's summer-sunset lighting and post-processing, balancing warm oranges and pinks against gameplay readability.

Environment

Rebuilding Hatbill Park as a playable world

The environment was based on a recognizable interpretation of the real Hatbill Park area in Florida.

A modified satellite reference was converted into a semi-transparent material and placed over the Unity terrain at 1:1 scale so waterways and major features could be traced.

Unity Terrain Tools, vegetation and terrain-painting workflows were then used to build the finished environment.

Technical Art & Optimization

Preserving the look without sacrificing performance

As the environment became denser, I refined occlusion culling, LODs and vegetation density to balance visual quality against rendering cost.

The environment was also divided into smaller terrain chunks and scenes that could be loaded additively.

One severe FPS issue was traced to barbed-wire geometry on a campground fence. Isolating the geometry allowed the art asset to be corrected.
